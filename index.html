01The poster

How do the climates of Melbourne and Singapore affect plant stress, and where air-quality sensors should go?

Leaves · John Monash Science School

  • Over 26 days, nasturtium leaves kept the same shape in Melbourne-like (52–54%) and Singapore-like (90–96%) humidity.
  • In the first week, the leaves in dry air went duller and more of them died, but the colour difference didn't last.

Sensors · NUS High School

  • Placing PM2.5 sensors with the weather in mind made pollution maps 10–50% more accurate when sources are predictable.
  • Melbourne didn't benefit more than Singapore: how densely sensors cover a city mattered more.

02How we did it

Leaves · John Monash Science School

  1. Term 2, 2026Proposal with NUS High. Our first plan was much broader, covering smoke and haze, leaf pores and a laser beam. We cut it down to what we could test properly.
  2. 29 Jun – 16 AugPhase 1: field trips. We visited street trees four times at three EPA Victoria air-monitoring sites (Alphington, Footscray and the CBD) and photographed their leaves up close, at about 10×, to count particles on the surface with ImageJ and trace the veins.
  3. AugustThe optical box. We shone a light across a box at a sensor and burned a little smoke in a dish between them, to see how much the smoke and its PM2.5 concentration disrupt the light.
  4. Why we changedFicus leaves are oval with no fixed centre, and our field photos had no scale in them. A nasturtium leaf is round, with a hub in the middle that every landmark can be measured from, so for Phase 2 we brought the experiment indoors.
  5. 21 Sep – 16 OctPhase 2: two tanks, 26 days. We kept one tank dry like Melbourne and one humid like Singapore, and photographed every tagged leaf on grid paper each day: 263 photos in all.
  6. 18 OctPlanning the tests. Before we looked at the Day 17–26 leaves, we wrote down exactly which tests we'd run, so we couldn't just pick whichever result looked best.
  7. Late OctoberThe Leaf Landmarks app. We turned our measuring code into an app and checked it against 68 of our photos.
  8. NovemberThe poster. We made a joint poster with NUS High for ISSF 2026.

03Extra Results

Leaves · John Monash Science School

How reliable are our measurements?

  • When we measured the same photos again, leaf shape moved by only 0.012 (Procrustes distance). That's less than a leaf changes from one day to the next (0.030) and much less than the difference between two leaves (0.063).
  • Our automatic scale readings from the grid paper were within 1.35% of careful hand measurements (median of 89 photos).
  • Our Days 17–26 test could only reliably pick up differences of at least 0.032 in greenness, 1.2% a day in growth and 0.029 in shape, so "no difference" means nothing bigger than that.

04Takeaways and next steps

What we'd do differently

  • Control the temperature and use more tanks. The humid tank also ran hotter (48 °C against 39 °C), so heat and humidity got mixed up.
  • Tag more leaves at the start. We had to replace 4 of the 5 dry-tank leaves as they died.

Next steps and future research

  • In the lab: hold the temperature steady, and test young, still-growing leaves.
  • In real cities: check street-tree leaves for the same first-week colour drop.
  • Smoke and haze: test PM2.5 with the optical box alongside the leaves.
  • Leaves and sensors together: compare leaf colour with sensor readings at the sites a weather-informed network picks.

05Leaf Landmarks app

We turned our measuring method into a free Windows app. You click a leaf's vein hub, and it finds the outline, places the 13 landmarks, reads the scale off the grid paper and measures size, shape and greenness.

For nasturtium leaves only (for now). You click the vein hub yourself on every photo; everything after that click is automatic.
